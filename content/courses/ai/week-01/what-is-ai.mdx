## Four definitions, one grid

The lecture gives one definition:

> Artificial Intelligence (AI) refers to the simulation of human intelligence in machines, enabling them to perform tasks that typically require human intelligence, such as learning, reasoning, problem-solving, and perception.

The textbook sorts every definition by two questions. Is AI about **thought** or about **behaviour**? And is it measured against **humans**, or against an ideal standard of **rationality**, doing the right thing given what you know?

<AiFourApproaches />

The lecture's definition, "simulation of *human* intelligence", sits in the human column. Russell and Norvig build their book, and so this course, on the bottom-right cell: **AI is the study of agents that act rationally.** Lecture 2 starts there. For a backend engineer the distinction is familiar: you do not test a service by checking that it reasons like you would. You check its outputs against a spec. The rational-agent view is the spec view.

The slide also defines **intelligence**: "the computational part of the ability to achieve goals in the world." That sentence is John McCarthy's, the man who named the field, and it is a rational-agent definition: intelligence is measured by goals achieved, not by resemblance to a person.

## The Turing test

In 1950 Alan Turing published "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" in the journal *Mind*. Instead of arguing about what "think" means, he proposed a game you can actually run.

<TuringTestFigure />

The slide splits it into four parts, and quizzes like to ask for them in that order:

1. **Setup.** A human judge holds text conversations through a terminal and does not know who is on the other end.
2. **Participants.** One human respondent and one machine.
3. **Goal.** The machine tries to answer so convincingly that the judge cannot reliably tell it from the human.
4. **Evaluation.** If the judge cannot consistently identify the machine, the machine passes.

**Why it mattered**, per the slides: it set a benchmark for machine intelligence, it pushed research into natural language processing, understanding and generation, and it started a debate about what "truly intelligent" means that still runs.

In systems terms, the Turing test is a **black-box acceptance test**. It checks behaviour at the interface and ignores the implementation, which is exactly why critics say it measures imitation rather than understanding.

<Deeper kind="edge" title="Criticisms, and the 2025 result">

- **The Chinese room** (John Searle, 1980). A person who speaks no Chinese follows a rulebook to answer Chinese questions perfectly. The room passes; nobody inside understands. Searle's claim: passing the test shows symbol manipulation, not understanding.
- **Humans are easy to fool.** ELIZA (1966) was a page of pattern-matching rules, and people confided in it. Passing can reflect the judge's gullibility more than the machine's ability.
- **The total Turing test** adds a video signal and objects to hand over, so the machine also needs vision and robotics. Russell and Norvig use it to list the six capabilities a humanlike AI would need: natural language processing, knowledge representation, automated reasoning, machine learning, computer vision and robotics.
- **It finally happened.** In a pre-registered three-party study published in 2025, GPT-4.5 told to adopt a humanlike persona was judged to be the human 73% of the time, more often than the real humans. ELIZA scored 23%. Whether that says more about the machines or about the test is the same debate Turing started.

</Deeper>

## Human intelligence vs AI

The lecture contrasts the two. Read this as a table of where each side wins.

| Humans are strong at | Machines are strong at |
|---|---|
| **Adaptability**: learning from experience and changing behaviour | **Speed and accuracy** of computation |
| **Creativity**: new ideas and solutions | **Repetitive tasks** without fatigue |
| **Emotional intelligence**: reading and managing emotions | **Large datasets**: patterns no person could scan |
| **Common sense** and **contextual understanding** | Consistency: the same input gives the same answer |
| **Social interaction**: communication, empathy, relationships | |
| Handling ambiguity, moral and ethical judgement | |

Humans have limits too: cognitive biases, errors in judgement, slow processing. And AI lacks consciousness and emotions. The slide's closing quote is Fei-Fei Li's: AI is "a tool to assist and augment human intelligence", not a substitute for it.

The slides list ten **limitations of AI**. Ten unconnected bullets are hard to recall under pressure, so learn them in four groups:

| Group | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **Knowing** | Lack of common sense and contextual understanding · generalisation challenges |
| **Data** | Data dependency · bias and fairness |
| **Trust** | Interpretability (deep models are "black boxes") · ethical and privacy concerns · security risks |
| **Cost** | Energy consumption · high development costs |
| | Plus: human–machine collaboration, making people and AI work well together |

The slides ask "Can AI replace human intelligence?" and leave it as a debate. For the rest of the course the working answer is narrower: build **agents** that act rationally in a defined environment. Deep Blue did that for chess. Lecture 2 makes "agent" and "rational" precise.

<Deeper kind="exam" title="Model answer: define AI and describe the Turing test (5 marks)">

**Definition (1 mark).** AI is the simulation of human intelligence in machines so they can perform tasks that normally need human intelligence, such as learning, reasoning, problem-solving and perception. For a stronger answer, add: definitions differ on two axes, thinking versus acting and human versus rational, and the textbook adopts "acting rationally", the study of rational agents.

**Turing test (3 marks).** Proposed by Alan Turing in "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950). A human judge holds text-only conversations with two hidden respondents, one human and one machine. The machine tries to make its answers indistinguishable from the human's. If the judge cannot reliably tell which is the machine, the machine is said to pass.

**Significance (1 mark).** It gave a concrete benchmark for machine intelligence, drove work on natural language processing, and started the lasting debate about what counts as real intelligence. The criticism worth one line: it tests imitation of behaviour, not understanding (Searle's Chinese room).

</Deeper>
