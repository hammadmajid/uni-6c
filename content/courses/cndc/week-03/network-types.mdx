Lectures 6 and 7 classify networks four ways: by **area**, by the **role** the machines play, by **topology** and by **transmission medium**. This lesson covers the first two. Topology and media each get a lesson of their own.

## By area: PAN, LAN, CAN, MAN, WAN

<NetworkScales />

- **PAN** (personal area network): the devices around one person, such as a phone, earbuds and a smartwatch, over Bluetooth or USB.
- **LAN** (local area network): one site, such as an office, a lab or a building. It is privately owned and easy to design and troubleshoot. Machines share files and printers.
- **CAN** (campus area network): several LANs linked across a limited area. The campus owner owns almost all the switches, routers and fibre.
- **MAN** (metropolitan area network): a city. The lecture's example is a cable TV network. Today it would be an ISP's metro fibre ring.
- **WAN** (wide area network): countries and continents, built on links you lease from carriers: leased lines, MPLS circuits, satellite, subsea fibre. The Internet is the largest WAN, a network of networks.

### The comparison table, and where the slide goes wrong

This is the lecture's table with two rows corrected:

| Parameter | LAN | MAN | WAN |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ownership | Private | Private or public | Private or public (usually leased) |
| Area covered | Small: a building or site | Moderate: a city | Very large: country, continent |
| Design and maintenance | Easy | Not easy | Not easy |
| Typical medium | Twisted pair, Wi-Fi (the slide says coax) | Fibre, coax, wireless | Leased fibre, PSTN lines, satellite |
| Data rate per link | High: 1 to 10 Gbps to your desk | Moderate | Lower per connection, and costs more per Mbps |
| Delay and errors | Low | Moderate | Higher: long propagation, more hops |

<Callout type="exam" title="The slide's version of the table">
The slide has two rows that contradict each other: **"Bandwidth: LAN Low, WAN High, MAN moderate"** and **"Data rates (speed): LAN High, WAN Low, MAN moderate"**. Bandwidth and data rate move together, so both rows cannot be right. The data-rate row is the one that matches reality: a LAN gives you gigabits, while the WAN link your office rents is slower and dearer per Mbps. A WAN backbone does carry huge aggregate capacity, which may be what the "High" was reaching for. If you are asked to reproduce the table, copy the slide's rows. If you are asked to *compare*, write "LAN: high data rate, low delay, low error rate; WAN: lower data rate per connection, higher delay", and add a one-line note about the slide's bandwidth row if you like. The slide's "LAN medium: coaxial cable" dates from 1980s bus Ethernet; today it is twisted pair and Wi-Fi.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="“In a LAN all machines are connected to a single cable”">
The slide says this. It was true for 10BASE5 and 10BASE2 Ethernet, which used a coax bus, until about 1990. A modern LAN is a **star**: every machine has its own cable to a switch. The next lesson shows why the industry switched.
</Callout>

## By role: peer-to-peer or client-server

<NetworkRoles />

In a **peer-to-peer** network every computer makes its own resources available and runs its own security. There is no server and no central control. That makes it cheap and quick to set up, and the lecture puts it at "fewer than 10 computers on a single LAN". It is not very secure, backups have no central home, and a PC that shares a busy printer slows down for its owner.

In a **client-server** network some machines are servers, which host and centrally manage resources and security. The rest are clients that request them. It is secure, reliable and has central backup, but it needs dedicated hardware and software and someone who knows how to administer them.

Types of server, as the lecture lists them:

| Server | Job |
|---|---|
| File server | Store, retrieve and move files for users |
| Print server | Queue and manage printing (and, on the slide, fax) |
| Application server | Share expensive software and computing power |
| Message server | Coordinate communication between users and applications: mail, chat |
| Database server | A kind of application server that gives users access to a central database |

<Callout type="exam" title="“Local area network (LAN) is based on client server network relationship”">
The slide says this, but a LAN can be either. Role and area are independent axes: a home LAN is usually peer-to-peer, and an office LAN with a domain controller is client-server. If the question quotes the slide, agree with it. If it asks you to classify, give both axes separately. The slide's "peer networks are amplified into home group" refers to Windows HomeGroup, which Microsoft removed in 2018.
</Callout>

<Deeper kind="beyond" title="Peer-to-peer means something bigger in week 4">

The lecture's peer-to-peer is a *LAN workgroup*: a few PCs sharing folders. In week 4 the same words mean an *application architecture*: BitTorrent, where millions of peers each upload what they download. There the "fewer than 10 computers" limit is backwards. Peer-to-peer scales *better* than client-server for file distribution, because every new peer brings its own upload capacity. The idea is the same (no dedicated server, every node both serves and requests), but the scale and the conclusion are opposite. Watch the context of the question.

</Deeper>
