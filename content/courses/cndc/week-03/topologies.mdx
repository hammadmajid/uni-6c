## The four shapes

**Bus.** One cable, the *trunk* or backbone, with every machine tapped onto it. It is cheap and simple and uses the least cable. Only one machine can send at a time, because everyone shares the one medium. Machines only listen; they do not regenerate the signal. One break in the trunk takes down the whole bus, and a fault is hard to locate.

**Star.** Every machine has its own cable to a central device. It uses more cable than a bus, but a broken cable affects only its own machine, and adding one is just plugging in. The centre is a single point of failure. Whether two machines can send at once depends on that central box, which is the next section.

**Ring.** Each machine connects to the next, and the last back to the first. Every machine **repeats** the signal on to its neighbour, so the signal is regenerated at each hop and a ring can span more distance than a bus. Who sends is decided by **token passing**: only the holder of the token may transmit. The drawbacks are that it is hard to add a machine without breaking the ring, and in a simple ring one failed machine or cable stops everything. FDDI's answer was a second ring running the other way: after a fault, traffic wraps around onto it.

**Mesh.** Every machine has a dedicated link to every other one. A failed link costs nothing, because traffic goes around it. It is robust and private, and many pairs can talk at once. The price is cabling that grows with the square of the number of machines.

<Callout type="key-idea" title="The mesh formula">
A full mesh of **n** devices needs **n(n − 1)/2** duplex links, and each device needs **n − 1** ports. For 8 devices that is 28 cables and 7 ports on every machine. That is why nobody full-meshes PCs. Full or partial mesh is used between a few core routers, where the redundancy is worth paying for.
</Callout>

## Hub or switch: what the star really is

The lecture says the star's central device is a hub, and that "depending on the intelligence of the hub, two or more computers may send at the same time". Those are really two different devices:

- A **hub** is a layer 1 repeater. Every bit that arrives on one port goes out of all the others. Electrically the star is still a bus: one sender at a time, everyone hears everything. People call it a *physical star, logical bus*.
- A **switch** is a layer 2 device. It learns which MAC address sits behind which port and forwards each frame only to its destination. PC 1 to PC 2 and PC 3 to PC 4 can then run at full speed simultaneously.

A modern Ethernet LAN is a star built on switches. Hubs are obsolete, and so are coax buses. The ring survives as a logical idea in some metro fibre networks, and mesh survives in the core and in Wi-Fi mesh systems.

<Deeper kind="why" title="Why shared media need an access protocol">

On a bus, or a star with a hub, two machines that transmit at the same moment garble each other: a **collision**. Something has to decide who talks. Classic Ethernet listened before sending and backed off after a collision (CSMA/CD). Token Ring avoided collisions by passing permission around. That decision is **medium access control**, and it gets all of weeks 9 and 10. A switched star mostly makes the problem go away, because each cable connects only two devices.

</Deeper>

<Deeper kind="edge" title="Tree and hybrid topologies">

The slide mentions tree in passing. A **tree** is stars of stars: the building switch feeds a floor switch, which feeds the room switches. It is what every real office network looks like. A **hybrid** mixes shapes, such as star LANs joined by a mesh of core routers. If an exam asks for "any two other topologies", give tree and hybrid with one line each.

</Deeper>
