The edge is the part of the network you can physically touch: hosts, and the access networks that connect them to the first router. This lesson is short on theory and heavy on classification, because "explain three access technologies with diagrams" is a stock 5-mark question.

It depends on whether the **last mile is shared**.

- **Cable (HFC)**: coax from the neighbourhood node is a shared broadcast medium. Every active home on that segment divides the downstream channel. Yes, the kid affects you.
- **Fiber PON**: one fiber from the central office is split passively to around 32 homes. Downstream is broadcast to all; each home's terminal keeps only its own frames. Capacity is shared, but it is 2.5 to 10 Gbps, so you rarely notice.
- **DSL**: your copper phone line runs only to your house. The kid cannot touch your last mile. But DSL is slow, and past the DSLAM everyone is sharing again.

The general rule: contention can appear at the first point where your traffic meets other people's. The technology decides where that point is.

</Predict>

## End systems, clients, servers

Hosts sit at the edge. They are split loosely into **clients** (laptops, phones, IoT devices that initiate requests) and **servers** (machines that wait for requests and serve content). Most servers today live in **data centers**: tens of thousands of hosts, each with its own fast access, connected by a tree of switches that week 9 examines. Your VPS is one such host. "Client" and "server" are roles, not hardware: your laptop runs a local dev server and is both.

## Access networks

An **access network** is the network that physically connects an end system to the first router on the path to any other distant end system. That first router is called the **edge router**. Three families:

Three details the exam likes:

- **DSL uses FDM on the phone line**: voice below 4 kHz, upstream in one band, downstream in a wider band. That is why it is asymmetric and why the phone still works while you download.
- **Cable is asymmetric too**, and because the coax is shared, the CMTS needs a medium access protocol to decide who transmits upstream when. That is a week 10 idea appearing early.
- **PON's splitter is passive**: no power, no electronics. The OLT does all the scheduling. Downstream is broadcast; upstream is time-shared.

### Enterprise and campus access

Offices and universities run **Ethernet**: twisted pair from each desk to a switch, at 100 Mbps to 10 Gbps, with switches uplinked to an institutional router that connects to the ISP. Alongside it, **Wi-Fi** (IEEE 802.11) gives wireless access within a few tens of metres of an access point, and the access point plugs into the same wired Ethernet. Wi-Fi is a shared medium: everyone on the same channel near the same access point competes. Week 12 covers how.

### Wide-area wireless access

**4G LTE and 5G**: the phone talks by radio to a base station operated by a cellular provider, which can be tens of kilometres away. Rates from tens of Mbps up to a gigabit in ideal 5G conditions. The cell is shared.

## Physical media, briefly

Bits travel as electromagnetic waves or optical pulses across a **physical medium**. Two groups:

- **Guided media**: waves travel inside a solid. Twisted-pair copper (UTP, the Cat 5e and Cat 6 in every office), coaxial cable, fiber optic cable.
- **Unguided media**: waves propagate through the atmosphere or space. Terrestrial radio (Wi-Fi, cellular, microwave links) and satellite.

Fiber wins on every physical metric: immune to electromagnetic interference, very low attenuation so it spans 100 km without repeaters, and rates in the tens of Tbps per fiber. Its only downsides are cost of the optical equipment and that you cannot bend it around a tight corner. Week 3 goes deeper into media; for now you need the guided versus unguided split and fiber's properties.

<Deeper kind="why" title="Why DSL gets slower with distance and cable does not">

Copper attenuates high frequencies far more than low ones, and DSL puts data in the high-frequency bands. Over a few kilometres of thin telephone wire, the usable band shrinks and the modem negotiates a lower rate. That is a physical fact about copper and there is no protocol trick around it, only shortening the copper by pushing fiber closer to the home (which is what "fiber to the cabinet" means).

Cable coax is thicker, better shielded, and the fiber already reaches the neighbourhood node, so the coax run is short. Cable rates are limited by sharing, not distance.

</Deeper>

<Deeper kind="beyond" title="The numbers behind DOCSIS and PON">

- **DOCSIS 3.1** (the cable standard) allocates downstream channels using orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing across up to 1.2 GHz of coax spectrum, giving around 10 Gbps of shared downstream on a segment. Upstream gets a much narrower band, hence asymmetry.
- **GPON** gives 2.488 Gbps downstream and 1.244 Gbps upstream on one fiber, split up to 1:64. Divide by the split ratio for the guaranteed floor per home; in practice most homes are idle so the effective rate is far higher.
- **XGS-PON** is the symmetric 10 Gbps successor being deployed now.

You do not need these for the exam. You do need them to have an opinion when your ISP quotes you a number.

</Deeper>
