The edge is the part of the network you can physically touch: hosts, and the access networks that connect them to the first router. This lesson is short on theory and heavy on classification, because "explain three access technologies with diagrams" is a stock 5-mark question. It depends on whether the **last mile is shared**.

- **Cable (HFC)**: the coax from the neighbourhood node is a broadcast medium. Every active home on the segment divides the downstream channel. Yes, the kid affects you.
- **Fiber PON**: one fiber is split passively to around 32 homes. Capacity is shared, but it is 2.5 to 10 Gbps, so you rarely notice.
- **DSL**: your copper pair runs only to your house. The kid cannot touch your last mile. Past the DSLAM everyone is sharing again.

Contention appears at the first point where your traffic meets other people's. The technology decides where that point is. Flip through the figure below and watch where the amber starts.

</Predict>

## End systems, clients, servers

Hosts sit at the edge, split loosely into **clients** (laptops, phones, IoT devices that initiate requests) and **servers** (machines that wait for requests). Most servers live in **data centers**, each host with its own fast link into a tree of switches that week 9 examines. "Client" and "server" are roles, not hardware: your laptop running a local dev server is both.

## Access networks

An **access network** physically connects an end system to the first router on the path to any distant end system, the **edge router**. The exam wants three home technologies with the medium, the box at the ISP end, and whether the last mile is shared.

<Callout type="exam" title="Three details the exam likes">
**DSL uses FDM on the phone line**: voice below 4 kHz, upstream in one narrow band, downstream in a wider band, hence asymmetric, and the phone works while you download. **Cable's coax is shared**, so the CMTS needs a medium access protocol to decide who transmits upstream when (a week 10 idea appearing early). **PON's splitter is passive**: no power, no electronics; the OLT does all the scheduling.

### Enterprise, campus, and wide-area wireless

| Where | Technology | Medium | Shared? | Rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Office, university | **Ethernet** | Twisted pair from desk to switch, switches uplinked to the institutional router | No, one link per port | 100 Mbps to 10 Gbps |
| Same building | **Wi-Fi** (IEEE 802.11) | Radio within tens of metres of an access point, which plugs into the wired Ethernet | Yes, everyone on the same channel near the same AP | Tens of Mbps to Gbps |
| Anywhere in range | **4G LTE / 5G** | Radio to a base station, possibly tens of km away | Yes, the cell | Tens of Mbps to a gigabit in ideal 5G |

## Physical media

Bits travel as electromagnetic waves or light pulses across a **physical medium**. **Guided** media carry the wave inside a solid: twisted-pair copper (the Cat 5e and Cat 6 in every office), coax, fiber. **Unguided** media radiate through the atmosphere or space: terrestrial radio and satellite. Week 3 goes deeper; for now you need the split and where fiber sits.

<MediaChart />

<Callout type="key-idea" title="Fiber wins on every physical metric">
Immune to electromagnetic interference (it carries light, not current), very low attenuation so it spans 100 km without repeaters, and tens of Tbps per strand. Its only costs are the optical equipment at each end and the bend radius.
</Callout>

<Deeper kind="why" title="Why DSL gets slower with distance and cable does not">

Copper attenuates high frequencies far more than low ones, and DSL puts data in the high-frequency bands. Over a few kilometres of thin telephone wire the usable band shrinks and the modem negotiates a lower rate. No protocol trick fixes physics; the only remedy is shorter copper, which is what "fiber to the cabinet" means. Cable coax is thicker, better shielded, and the fiber already reaches the neighbourhood node, so the coax run is short. Cable rates are limited by sharing, not distance.

</Deeper>

<Deeper kind="beyond" title="The numbers behind DOCSIS and PON">

- **DOCSIS 3.1** (cable) uses OFDM across up to 1.2 GHz of coax spectrum for around 10 Gbps of shared downstream per segment. Upstream gets a much narrower band, hence asymmetry.
- **GPON**: 2.488 Gbps down, 1.244 Gbps up on one fiber, split up to 1:64. Divide by the split ratio for the guaranteed floor per home.
- **XGS-PON**: the symmetric 10 Gbps successor being deployed now.

Not examinable. Useful when your ISP quotes you a number.

</Deeper>
