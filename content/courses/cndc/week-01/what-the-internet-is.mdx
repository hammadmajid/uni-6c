You have deployed services behind load balancers, so half of this lesson is already in your head. The other half is vocabulary the exam expects verbatim, plus one definition that most students get subtly wrong.

Most people say "one request." It is at least four conversations, each governed by a different protocol:

1. **DNS**: your laptop asks a resolver for the IP address of example.com (UDP, port 53).
2. **TCP handshake**: SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK to that IP on port 443.
3. **TLS handshake**: certificates and keys, inside the TCP connection.
4. **HTTP**: finally `GET /`. Then the bytes.

Every one of those crosses the same physical path, typically 10 to 20 hops. Each hop looks at the packet, decides, forwards. That sentence is the whole course. The figure below is that path.

## Two views of the same thing

Exams ask for both, usually as "describe the Internet from a nuts-and-bolts view and from a services view."

<InternetMap />

<Callout type="key-idea" title="Nuts-and-bolts view: what the Internet is made of">
<Term term="Host (end system)" def="A device at the edge of the network that runs applications. Also called an end system. Hosts are clients or servers.">**Hosts**</Term> run applications. <Term term="Communication link" def="A physical medium connecting two nodes: copper, fiber, or radio. Characterised by its transmission rate in bits per second.">**Communication links**</Term> (copper, fiber, radio) join nodes; a link's headline number is its **transmission rate in bits per second**, never bytes. <Term term="Packet switch" def="A device that takes a packet arriving on one link and forwards it out another. Routers (network core) and link-layer switches (access networks).">**Packet switches**</Term> (routers in the core, switches in LANs) forward packets link to link. <Term term="ISP" def="Internet Service Provider. A network of packet switches and links that provides access to end systems. ISPs are themselves interconnected in tiers.">**ISPs**</Term> are networks of those, interconnected in tiers. **Protocols** (TCP, IP, HTTP, 802.11) govern every exchange, and **standards** bodies write them down: the IETF publishes RFCs, the IEEE does 802.x.
</Callout>

A host with data to send chops it into **packets**, adds header bytes to each, and pushes them onto its link. Switches forward, the receiver reassembles. Nothing in the network reserves capacity for you unless a very particular design decision was made, which is next week's topic.

<Callout type="key-idea" title="Services view: what the Internet offers">
An **infrastructure that provides services to distributed applications**: programs on different end systems exchanging data. The interface is the **socket interface**, the rules a program follows to hand data to the Internet for delivery to a program on another host. You have called `listen()`, `accept()` and `connect()`; that is it. The textbook's analogy is the postal service: follow the envelope rules and it delivers. You never see the sorting facility.
</Callout>

## What a protocol is

This is the one definition that gets marked down constantly, because students write "a set of rules" and stop. The textbook definition has three parts, and graders look for all three. They are drawn on the picture.

<ProtocolExchange />

<Callout type="key-idea" title="Definition to memorise">
A protocol defines the **format** and the **order** of messages exchanged between two or more communicating entities, as well as the **actions** taken on the transmission and/or receipt of a message or other event.
</Callout>

<Deeper kind="why" title="Why the outline says 'protocols and algorithms'">

A protocol says who sends what, in what format, in what order. It does not say how each side decides. That is the algorithm:

- **Routing**: OSPF is the protocol (message formats, flooding rules). Dijkstra's shortest path is the algorithm each router runs on the data it collected.
- **Congestion control**: TCP is the protocol. Additive-increase, multiplicative-decrease is the algorithm deciding how fast to send.
- **Medium access**: CSMA/CA in Wi-Fi is the protocol. Binary exponential backoff is the algorithm that picks how long to wait after a collision.

Exam phrasing: the protocol makes independently written implementations interoperate; the algorithm makes each participant's decisions good. A compliant protocol with a terrible algorithm still works, just badly.

</Deeper>

<Deeper kind="history" title="Who runs the Internet">

Nobody, and that is the exam answer too. ISPs interconnect by private agreement (peering or paid transit). The IETF publishes standards but cannot force anyone to follow them; people follow them because otherwise their equipment does not talk to anyone else's. ICANN coordinates names and numbers. Coordination, not control. The result is a federation of roughly 75,000 autonomous systems agreeing only on the protocols at the seams. Week 6 is about routing across that federation.

</Deeper>

## Where you are in the picture

Look back at the map. Your home connects through an **access ISP**; access ISPs buy transit from **regional ISPs**; those connect to **tier-1 ISPs** that span continents and peer with each other for free. Content providers run their own global networks and plug into access ISPs at **Internet exchange points**, skipping the tiers. Your SSH session to Frankfurt crosses the hierarchy twice.
