This is the most examined lesson in the course. Every paper has at least one delay numerical, and the errors are always the same: bits versus bytes, kilometres versus metres, and confusing the two delays that happen to be measured in the same unit.

<Predict
  id="which-is-longer"
  prompt="A 1000-byte packet is sent over a 1 Mbps link that is 1 km long. Which takes longer: pushing all the bits onto the link, or the first bit travelling to the far end? By roughly what factor?"
  answer="Pushing the bits (transmission delay) takes 8 ms. The first bit's trip (propagation delay) takes about 5 µs. Transmission is about 1600 times longer here. Change the link to 100 Gbps over 5000 km and propagation dominates instead."
>

| | Formula | Numbers | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transmission | L ÷ R | 8000 bits ÷ 1,000,000 bits/s | **8 ms** |
| Propagation | d ÷ s | 1000 m ÷ 200,000,000 m/s | **5 µs** |

Transmission wins by 1600×. Most people guess the opposite because "a signal down a wire" sounds like the slow part. Now flip it: the same packet on a 100 Gbps link across 5000 km is 80 ns of transmission against 25 ms of propagation. The two delays depend on different things and neither cares about the other.

</Predict>

## Nodal delay

Every node on the path (host or router) adds four delays. Their sum is the **total nodal delay**, and the exam wants the table verbatim:

| Delay | What it is | Depends on |
|---|---|---|
| Processing, d_proc | Read the header, check for bit errors, pick the output link | Router hardware, usually microseconds |
| Queueing, d_queue | Wait in the output buffer behind earlier packets | Congestion: the only variable one |
| Transmission, d_trans | Push all L bits onto the link at rate R | **L ÷ R** |
| Propagation, d_prop | The first bit crosses the physical length of the link | **d ÷ s** |

<CaravanDiagram />

<Callout type="key-idea" title="d_nodal = d_proc + d_queue + d_trans + d_prop">
**s** is the propagation speed in the medium, 2×10⁸ to 3×10⁸ m/s. Fiber and copper are both about 2×10⁸, two thirds of c. If a question does not give s, state your assumption and use it consistently.
</Callout>

## End-to-end delay across N links

Each router must **store the whole packet before forwarding** it, so a packet crossing N links is transmitted N times. With identical links: **d_end-to-end = N × (L ÷ R + d ÷ s)**. If the links differ, add up each link's own L ÷ R and d ÷ s.

<WorkedExample title="Three links, cross-country">
  <Step label="Set up">
    L = 1500 bytes, R = 10 Mbps on every link, N = 3 links, each link d = 1000 km, s = 2×10⁸ m/s.
  </Step>
  <Step label="Convert units first" tryFirst="Convert L to bits and d to metres before anything else.">
    L = 1500 × 8 = **12,000 bits**. d = 1000 km = **1,000,000 m**. R = **10,000,000 bits/s**.
  </Step>
  <Step label="Per-link transmission delay" tryFirst="L / R">
    12,000 ÷ 10,000,000 = 0.0012 s = **1.2 ms**.
  </Step>
  <Step label="Per-link propagation delay" tryFirst="d / s">
    1,000,000 ÷ 200,000,000 = 0.005 s = **5 ms**.
  </Step>
  <Step label="Sum over three links">
    3 × (1.2 + 5) = 3 × 6.2 = **18.6 ms**. Propagation dominates: three cross-country hops of fiber cost more than three retransmissions of the packet.
  </Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="The three unit traps">
**Bytes to bits**: multiply by 8. **km to m**: multiply by 1000. **M means 10⁶** in networking, never 2²⁰: 10 Mbps = 10,000,000 bits/s. Do the conversions in a separate first line of your answer; graders give method marks for it and it stops the errors at the source.
</Callout>

## Queueing delay and traffic intensity

The one delay with no closed formula: it depends on how many packets are already waiting when yours arrives. The textbook gives you one dimensionless number to reason with, the ratio of the rate bits arrive to the rate bits can leave.

<IntensityCurve />

<Callout type="key-idea" title="Traffic intensity = L × a ÷ R">
a is the average arrival rate in packets per second, L bits per packet, R the link rate. Design rule: **keep it below 1**, and in practice below about 0.7, which is why ISPs upgrade links long before they are full. Even a low average does not save you from a burst: N packets arriving together make the k-th one wait (k−1) × L ÷ R.
</Callout>

<QueueingLab />

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Loss is the same curve, one step later">
Buffers are finite. When a packet arrives to a full buffer the router drops it, and from the sender's view it simply vanished. TCP notices and retransmits (week 5); UDP does not care. Loss rate climbs with traffic intensity exactly as delay does.
</Callout>

<Deeper kind="why" title="Why two thirds of c, and why it matters for your VPS">

Light in glass travels slower than in vacuum because the refractive index of silica fiber is about 1.47: c ÷ 1.47 ≈ 2.04×10⁸ m/s. Electrical signals in copper are similar. Nothing in engineering gets around this.

Islamabad to Frankfurt is about 5000 km as the crow flies and maybe 8000 km as the fiber runs (cables go around, not through). At 2×10⁸ m/s that is 40 ms one way, 80 ms round trip, before any router touches the packet. When your SSH session feels laggy at 100 ms, roughly 80% of it is physics. The other 20 ms is queueing and processing across 15 to 20 hops. The only fix for the physics is to move the server.

</Deeper>

<Deeper kind="beyond" title="Bufferbloat">

Cheap home routers and modems shipped with enormous buffers on the theory that dropping packets is bad. The result: under load, traffic intensity sits near 1 and every packet waits hundreds of milliseconds in a huge queue instead of being dropped early so TCP could slow down. That is bufferbloat, and it is why a single upload can make your video call stutter. Modern queue management (CoDel, fq_codel, CAKE) deliberately keeps queues short. If you run OpenWrt on a router, you have almost certainly enabled it.

</Deeper>

<Callout type="exam" title="Your formula sheet for this topic">
d_nodal = d_proc + d_queue + d_trans + d_prop. d_trans = L/R. d_prop = d/s. End-to-end over N identical links: N(L/R + d/s). Traffic intensity = La/R; keep below 1. Convert bytes to bits and km to m on line one of every answer.
</Callout>
