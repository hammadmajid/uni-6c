This is the most examined lesson in the course. Every paper has at least one delay numerical, and the errors are always the same: bits versus bytes, kilometres versus metres, and confusing the two delays that happen to be measured in the same unit.

<Predict
  id="which-is-longer"
  prompt="A 1000-byte packet is sent over a 1 Mbps link that is 1 km long. Which takes longer: pushing all the bits onto the link, or the first bit travelling to the far end? By roughly what factor?"
  answer="Pushing the bits (transmission delay) takes 8 ms. The first bit's trip (propagation delay) takes about 5 µs. Transmission is about 1600 times longer here. Change the link to 100 Gbps over 5000 km and propagation dominates instead."
>

**Transmission delay** = L / R = 8000 bits ÷ 1,000,000 bits/s = **8 ms**.

**Propagation delay** = d / s = 1000 m ÷ 200,000,000 m/s = **5 µs**.

Transmission wins by a factor of 1600. Most people guess the opposite because "sending a signal down a wire" sounds like the slow part.

Now flip it. A 1000-byte packet on a 100 Gbps link across 5000 km: transmission is 80 ns, propagation is 25 ms. Propagation wins by 300,000×.

The two delays depend on completely different things. Transmission on packet size and link rate; propagation on distance and the speed of light in the medium. Neither one cares about the other. Keep them separate in your head and the numericals become easy.

</Predict>

## Nodal delay

As a packet travels from one node (host or router) to the next, it suffers four delays at each node. Their sum is the **total nodal delay**:

**d_nodal = d_proc + d_queue + d_trans + d_prop**

| Delay | What it is | Depends on | Typical size |
|---|---|---|---|
| Processing, d_proc | Examine the header, check bits for errors, decide the output link | Router hardware | Microseconds or less |
| Queueing, d_queue | Wait in the output buffer while earlier packets are transmitted | Congestion: how many packets are ahead | Zero to milliseconds; the variable one |
| Transmission, d_trans | Push all L bits of the packet onto the link at rate R | **L / R** | Microseconds to milliseconds |
| Propagation, d_prop | The first bit travels the physical length of the link | **d / s** | Microseconds to hundreds of ms |

**s** is the propagation speed in the medium: 2×10⁸ to 3×10⁸ m/s. Fiber and copper are both about 2×10⁸ (two thirds of c). If a question does not give s, state your assumption and use 2×10⁸ or 3×10⁸ consistently.

The caravan analogy from the textbook, since it fixes the distinction permanently: a caravan of 10 cars (bits) drives between tollbooths (routers) 100 km apart (links). Each tollbooth services one car in 12 seconds: that is transmission delay, the time to push the whole packet through. Between tollbooths the cars drive at 100 km/h: that is propagation delay, and it does not care how many cars there are.

## End-to-end delay across N links

Suppose N links between source and destination, each with rate R, and one packet of L bits. Ignore processing and queueing. Because each router must **store the whole packet before forwarding** it, the packet is transmitted N times, once per link:

**d_end-to-end = N × (L / R + d / s)**

Or with propagation ignored, simply **N × L / R**. If the links differ, sum each link's own L/R and d/s.

<WorkedExample title="Three links, cross-country">
  <Step label="Set up">
    L = 1500 bytes, R = 10 Mbps on every link, N = 3 links, each link d = 1000 km, s = 2×10⁸ m/s.
  </Step>
  <Step label="Convert units first" tryFirst="Convert L to bits and d to metres before anything else.">
    L = 1500 × 8 = **12,000 bits**. d = 1000 km = **1,000,000 m**. R = **10,000,000 bits/s**.
  </Step>
  <Step label="Per-link transmission delay" tryFirst="L / R">
    12,000 ÷ 10,000,000 = 0.0012 s = **1.2 ms**.
  </Step>
  <Step label="Per-link propagation delay" tryFirst="d / s">
    1,000,000 ÷ 200,000,000 = 0.005 s = **5 ms**.
  </Step>
  <Step label="Sum over three links">
    3 × (1.2 + 5) = 3 × 6.2 = **18.6 ms**. Propagation dominates: three cross-country hops of fiber cost more than three retransmissions of the packet.
  </Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="The three unit traps">
**Bytes to bits**: multiply by 8. **km to m**: multiply by 1000. **M means 10⁶** in networking, never 2²⁰: 10 Mbps = 10,000,000 bits/s. Do the conversions in a separate first line of your answer; graders give method marks for it and it stops the errors at the source.
</Callout>

## Queueing delay and traffic intensity

The one delay with no closed formula. It depends on how many packets are already waiting when yours arrives, which depends on the arrival pattern. The textbook gives you a single dimensionless number to reason with:

**Traffic intensity = L × a / R**

where a is the average arrival rate in packets per second, L bits per packet, R the link rate. It is the ratio of the rate bits arrive to the rate bits can leave.

- **La/R > 1**: bits arrive faster than they can be sent. The queue grows without bound. Delay heads to infinity, then the buffer fills and packets are dropped.
- **La/R ≤ 1**: it depends on the burstiness. Periodic arrivals spaced L/R apart wait zero. A burst of N packets arriving together makes the k-th packet wait (k−1)L/R even when the average is low.
- As **La/R approaches 1** from below, the average queueing delay grows sharply. This is the famous hockey-stick curve.

<QueueingLab />

<Callout type="key-idea">
Design rule from the textbook: **keep traffic intensity below 1**. That is why ISPs upgrade links at 70% average utilisation rather than 100%: the delay curve has already started climbing.
</Callout>

**Packet loss** follows directly. Buffers are finite. When a packet arrives to a full buffer, the router has nowhere to put it, so it drops it. From the sender's point of view the packet vanished. TCP notices (week 5) and retransmits; UDP does not care. Loss rate rises with traffic intensity just like delay does.

<Deeper kind="why" title="Why two thirds of c, and why it matters for your VPS">

Light in glass travels slower than in vacuum because the refractive index of silica fiber is about 1.47: c ÷ 1.47 ≈ 2.04×10⁸ m/s. Electrical signals in copper are similar. Nothing in engineering gets around this.

Islamabad to Frankfurt is about 5000 km as the crow flies and maybe 8000 km as the fiber runs (cables go around, not through). At 2×10⁸ m/s that is 40 ms one way, 80 ms round trip, before any router touches the packet. When your SSH session feels laggy at 100 ms, roughly 80% of it is physics. The other 20 ms is queueing and processing across 15 to 20 hops. The only fix for the physics is to move the server.

</Deeper>

<Deeper kind="beyond" title="Bufferbloat">

Cheap home routers and modems shipped with enormous buffers on the theory that dropping packets is bad. The result: under load, traffic intensity sits near 1 and every packet waits hundreds of milliseconds in a huge queue instead of being dropped early so TCP could slow down. That is bufferbloat, and it is why a single upload can make your video call stutter. Modern queue management (CoDel, fq_codel, CAKE) deliberately keeps queues short. If you run OpenWrt on a router, you have almost certainly enabled it.

</Deeper>
