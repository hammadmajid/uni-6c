## Long answers

<Predict
  id="cp2-four-delays"
  prompt="Explain the four components of nodal delay in a packet-switched network, stating what each depends on. (5 marks)"
  answer="Processing (header checks and output link lookup; depends on router speed), queueing (waiting for earlier packets; depends on congestion, characterised by traffic intensity La/R), transmission (L/R; depends on packet length and link rate), propagation (d/s; depends on link length and propagation speed). Total is their sum."
>

1. **Processing delay**: time to examine the packet header, check for bit errors and determine the output link. Depends on router hardware; typically microseconds.
2. **Queueing delay**: time waiting in the output buffer while packets ahead of it are transmitted. Depends on the level of congestion, characterised by traffic intensity La/R; ranges from zero to milliseconds, and grows sharply as La/R approaches 1.
3. **Transmission delay**: time to push all L bits of the packet onto the link at rate R; d_trans = L/R. Depends on packet length and link rate, not on distance.
4. **Propagation delay**: time for a bit to travel the physical length d of the link at speed s; d_prop = d/s. Depends on distance and medium, not on packet length or link rate.

Total nodal delay = d_proc + d_queue + d_trans + d_prop. Give one numerical example: a 1000-byte packet on a 1 Mbps, 1 km link has d_trans = 8 ms and d_prop = 5 µs.

</Predict>

<Predict
  id="cp2-switching-compare"
  prompt="Compare circuit switching and packet switching. (5 marks)"
  answer="Resources reserved vs on demand; setup phase vs none; guaranteed constant rate vs variable with queueing and possible loss; wasted idle capacity vs statistical multiplexing; suited to continuous vs bursty traffic. Example: 1 Mbps link, 100 kbps users active 10% of the time: 10 users vs 35."
>

Five contrasts, then the example:

| | Circuit switching | Packet switching |
|---|---|---|
| Resources | Reserved end to end for the session | On demand, no reservation |
| Setup | Required | None |
| Rate and delay | Guaranteed constant rate, fixed delay | Variable rate, queueing delay, possible loss |
| Efficiency | Idle reserved capacity is wasted | Statistical multiplexing reuses idle capacity |
| Best for | Continuous traffic (voice) | Bursty traffic (data) |

Example: on a 1 Mbps link with users needing 100 kbps 10% of the time, circuit switching supports 10 users; packet switching supports 35 with only a 0.04% chance of momentary overload.

</Predict>

Closing sentence: OSI is the conceptual reference; TCP/IP is the working architecture, with its layers mapping onto OSI's except that presentation and session are handled by applications.
