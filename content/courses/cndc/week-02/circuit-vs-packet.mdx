The network core is the mesh of routers and links that connects the edges. There are exactly two ways to move data through it. The telephone network picked one in 1880 and the Internet picked the other in 1969. This lesson makes you compute why.

<Predict
  id="stat-mux"
  prompt="A 1 Mbps link. Each user needs 100 kbps while active, and is active only 10% of the time. How many users can circuit switching support? How many can packet switching support if we accept a 0.04% chance that demand briefly exceeds the link?"
  answer="Circuit switching: 10 users (1 Mbps ÷ 100 kbps). Packet switching: 35 users, because the probability that more than 10 of 35 are active at once is about 0.0004."
>

**Circuit switching: 10 users.** Each gets a reserved 100 kbps slice whether or not they are using it. 1000 ÷ 100 = 10. The eleventh caller gets a busy signal.

**Packet switching: 35 users.** With 35 users each active 10% of the time, the chance that 11 or more are active simultaneously is around 0.0004. The rest of the time, everyone gets their full 100 kbps with no delay. Three and a half times the users on the same wire, at the cost of a tiny probability of a short queue.

That is **statistical multiplexing**, and it is the reason the Internet works economically. The lab below lets you push the numbers.

</Predict>

## Circuit switching

The resources needed for a session, **buffer space and link transmission rate**, are **reserved** for the whole session along the path. Three phases: **setup** (a path is chosen and every switch on it reserves capacity), **data transfer** at a guaranteed constant rate, and **teardown**.

The textbook analogy: a restaurant that takes reservations. You wait before you arrive, but once seated the table is yours regardless of how slowly you eat. Traditional telephone networks work this way: dialling is the setup phase, and a call gets a guaranteed 64 kbps whether you talk or sit in silence.

A link carries many circuits at once through one of two multiplexing schemes:

- <Term term="FDM" def="Frequency-Division Multiplexing. The link's frequency spectrum is divided into bands; each circuit owns one band for the duration of the connection.">**Frequency-division multiplexing (FDM)**</Term>: the spectrum is split into bands and each circuit owns a band. FM radio stations sharing the 88 to 108 MHz band is FDM. A phone circuit got 4 kHz of bandwidth.
- <Term term="TDM" def="Time-Division Multiplexing. Time is divided into frames of fixed duration, each frame into a fixed number of slots; each circuit owns one slot per frame.">**Time-division multiplexing (TDM)**</Term>: time is divided into frames, each frame into slots, and each circuit gets one slot in every frame. Its rate is the link rate divided by the number of slots.

<Callout type="key-idea">
Under TDM, a circuit's transmission rate is **frame rate × bits per slot**, which simplifies to **link rate ÷ number of slots**. With 24 slots on a 1.536 Mbps link, each circuit gets 64 kbps. That formula is a guaranteed exam numerical.
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="Sending 640 kbits over a TDM circuit (Kurose 1.3)">
  <Step label="Set up the numbers">
    File: 640,000 bits. Link: 1.536 Mbps with TDM using 24 slots per frame. Circuit setup takes 500 ms.
  </Step>
  <Step label="Compute the circuit's rate" tryFirst="Work out the per-circuit rate before revealing.">
    The circuit gets one of 24 slots, so its rate is 1.536 Mbps ÷ 24 = **64 kbps**.
  </Step>
  <Step label="Compute the transfer time" tryFirst="Now the time to push 640,000 bits at that rate.">
    640,000 bits ÷ 64,000 bits/s = **10 s**.
  </Step>
  <Step label="Add setup">
    10 s + 0.5 s setup = **10.5 s** total. Note that the link's 1.536 Mbps is irrelevant to your circuit; you only ever get your slot.
  </Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Packet switching

No reservation. The source chops the message into packets, and each packet travels through the links and switches on its own. At each switch, the packet is fully received and stored, then transmitted on the output link: **store-and-forward**. If the output link is busy, the packet waits in an **output buffer** (a queue). If the buffer is full, the packet is **dropped**: packet loss.

The textbook analogy: a restaurant with no reservations. You may wait for a table, but the restaurant seats far more people over an evening because no table sits empty waiting for a booking.

Resources are used **on demand**. When one user is silent, another user's packets flow. That is statistical multiplexing, and it is why the 35-user answer above beats 10.

<SwitchingLab />

<WorkedExample title="Where the 35 comes from">
  <Step label="Model it">
    Each of N users is active independently with probability p = 0.1. The number active at once is Binomial(N, 0.1). The link is overloaded when more than 10 are active.
  </Step>
  <Step label="Compute the overload probability for N = 35" tryFirst="What kind of number do you expect: 10%, 1%, 0.1%?">
    P(more than 10 of 35 active) = 1 − P(at most 10) ≈ **0.0004**. So 99.96% of the time, every active user gets the full 100 kbps and there is no queueing at all.
  </Step>
  <Step label="Compare">
    Circuit switching serves 10 users with a hard guarantee. Packet switching serves 35 with a 0.04% chance of brief queueing. For bursty traffic (which is almost all data traffic), packet switching wins by a wide margin.
  </Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Comparison

| | Circuit switching | Packet switching |
|---|---|---|
| Resources | Reserved end to end for the session | Used on demand; no reservation |
| Setup phase | Required before data flows | None; just send |
| Rate | Guaranteed, constant | Variable; shares with others |
| Idle capacity | Wasted (silent periods still reserved) | Reused by other users |
| Delay | Fixed after setup | Variable queueing delay |
| Loss | None once the circuit exists (but calls can be refused) | Possible when buffers overflow |
| Suits | Continuous traffic: voice, video with strict timing | Bursty traffic: web, email, file transfer, nearly everything |
| Complexity | Switches must track sessions | Switches are stateless per packet; simpler and cheaper |
| Example | Telephone network, 2G voice | The Internet |

<Deeper kind="why" title="Why the Internet's designers chose packets">

Three reasons, in order of importance:

1. **Data is bursty.** A terminal session in 1969, or a web page today, sends nothing for seconds and then a burst. Reserving a circuit for that is absurd.
2. **Simplicity.** A circuit switch must remember every active session. A packet switch needs only a forwarding table and a queue. Simpler switches are cheaper and fail more gracefully.
3. **Resilience.** With no per-session state in the core, a failed router just means packets take another path. A failed circuit switch drops every call through it.

The cost: no guarantees. Delay varies, packets are lost, and anything needing steady timing (voice, video) must cope in software. Week 13 is about how it copes.

</Deeper>

<Deeper kind="beyond" title="Virtual circuits: the hybrid you will meet in real networks">

Between the two extremes sits **virtual-circuit switching**: packets, but along a path set up in advance, with each switch keeping per-connection state. ATM did this in the 1990s; **MPLS** does it inside every large ISP today, labelling packets so core routers forward on a short label instead of a full IP lookup. Your traffic to a VPS almost certainly rides MPLS tunnels inside carrier networks without your ever seeing it.

</Deeper>
