Entities and attributes describe the things. This lesson covers the lines between them, how many of each can sit at the ends of a line, and which attributes identify an entity. That is the second half of lecture 3 (slides 21–22, 51 and 53–64), plus two ideas the lecture's learning outcomes promise but the slides only sketch: participation and weak entities.

## 1. Relationships, relationship types and relationship sets

A **relationship** is an association among entities: Ali ENROLLS_IN Databases. The slides draw it as a **diamond**, named with a verb in capitals, joined by lines to the entity types it links.

The same three-way split you learned for entities applies here:

| Entity side | Relationship side | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Entity type (rectangle) | **Relationship type** (diamond) | ENROLLS_IN between STUDENT and COURSE |
| Entity instance | **Relationship instance** | Ali enrolled in Databases |
| Entity set | **Relationship set** | every enrollment that exists right now |

Slide 22 defines a relationship set as a "collection of similar relationships", for example all enrollments in different courses. Each instance links exactly one entity from each participating type: one enrollment is one student plus one course. The number of entity types a relationship links is its **degree**. Almost everything you draw this term is **binary** (degree 2). A ternary relationship, such as SUPPLIER supplies PART to PROJECT, links three.

A relationship can have attributes of its own. **Grade** belongs to neither STUDENT (Ali has a different grade in every course) nor COURSE (every student gets a different grade), so in Elmasri it hangs off the ENROLLS_IN diamond as an oval.

## 2. Cardinality ratios: 1:1, 1:N, M:N

The **cardinality ratio** of a binary relationship is the maximum number of relationship instances an entity can take part in. There are three:

- **1:1** — a PERSON holds one PASSPORT, a PASSPORT belongs to one PERSON.
- **1:N** — a TEACHER teaches many SECTIONs, a SECTION has one TEACHER.
- **M:N** — a STUDENT enrolls in many COURSEs, a COURSE has many STUDENTs.

The explorer draws the Chen labels and, underneath, a set of real instances that obeys them. Switch the ratio and watch which dots are allowed more than one line.

<ErCardinalityExplorer />

What to notice: in **1:N** every dot on the N side has at most one line, and dots on the 1 side can fan out. In **M:N** both sides fan out. In **1:1** nobody fans out. The ratio is a statement about **maxima** only. It says nothing about whether an entity may have zero lines; that is the next section.

<Callout type="exam" title="1:M, M:M and 1:N, M:N are the same thing">
Slide 51 writes the ratios as **1:1, 1:M, M:M** ("A Teacher teaches many Students", "A Student enrolls in many Courses"). Slide 22 and Elmasri write **1:1, 1:N, M:N**. They mean the same. Use 1:N and M:N on your diagram: M:N makes clear that the two "many" sides are independent numbers, which M:M hides.
</Callout>

## 3. Participation: total or partial

Cardinality gives the maximum. **Participation** gives the minimum: must every entity take part in the relationship, or may some sit out?

- **Total participation** (also called **existence dependency**): every entity of that type must take part in at least one relationship instance. Drawn as a **double line** between the entity and the diamond.
- **Partial participation**: some entities may take part in none. Drawn as a **single line**.

Every SECTION must have a teacher, so SECTION's line is double. A teacher on sabbatical teaches nothing this term, so TEACHER's line is single. Go back to the explorer and toggle the two switches: an amber dot is an entity with no line at all, and it appears only on a partial side.

Cardinality ratio and participation together are what Elmasri calls the **structural constraints** of a relationship, the phrase on the lecture's summary slide.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="On the outcomes, barely on the slides">
Lecture 3's learning outcome 3 is "explain relationship types and multiplicity (1:1, 1:N, M:N) **and participation constraints**", and the summary slide lists "structural constraints on relationships". No slide shows the double-line notation. This section follows Elmasri chapter 3. Expect it on an ERD question even though it was not drawn in class.
</Callout>

<Deeper kind="formal" title="The (min, max) alternative">

Elmasri also gives a more precise notation: write a pair **(min, max)** on each line, meaning each entity of that type takes part in at least min and at most max relationship instances. min = 0 is partial participation and min ≥ 1 is total. The explorer prints both notations under the diagram.

Watch the direction: (min, max) is written on the entity's **own** line and counts that entity's participation. For TEACHER — TEACHES — SECTION, TEACHER gets (0, N) and SECTION gets (1, 1). That puts the "many" on the TEACHER side, the opposite of where the 1:N labels go. Use one notation per diagram and say which.

</Deeper>

## 4. Weak entities and identifying relationships

Slide 36 shows two symbols, a regular entity type and a **weak entity type** (a double rectangle), with the examples EMPLOYEE / DEPENDENTS and BOOK / BOOKCOPY. Slide 32 adds that a weak entity "depends on a strong entity", like an Order Item depending on its Order.

Elmasri's definition is sharper. A **weak entity type** has **no key attribute of its own**. Its entities are identified by being related to an entity of another type, the **owner** (or identifying) entity type, combined with some of their own attribute values.

<ErIdentifyingRelFigure />

Three symbols go with it, and a full-mark diagram uses all three:

1. **Double rectangle** for the weak entity (DEPENDENT).
2. **Double diamond** for the **identifying relationship** that ties it to its owner (DEPENDENTS_OF).
3. **Dashed underline** for the **partial key**, the attribute that tells two dependents of the same employee apart (Dependent_name).

A weak entity always has **total participation** in its identifying relationship: a dependent cannot exist without the employee it depends on. Its full identifier is the owner's key plus the partial key: Ssn and Dependent_name together.

## 5. Keys

A **key** is an attribute, or set of attributes, whose values identify each entity uniquely. In a Chen diagram the key attribute's name is **underlined** (the slide says "highlighted"). Slides 53 to 64 build a hierarchy of six kinds. Work through the lab before reading the definitions: the lab gives you the rules and the rows, and it is quicker to feel the difference than to memorise it.

<DbKeyFinderLab id="key-finder" />

Now the definitions, in the slides' order:

| Key | Definition | In the lab |
|---|---|---|
| **Super key** | Any set of attributes whose combined values uniquely identify each entity. May contain extra attributes. | reg_no and name together |
| **Candidate key** | A **minimal** super key: remove any attribute and it stops being unique. | reg_no, cnic, email |
| **Primary key** | The candidate key the designer chooses. Never NULL. | reg_no |
| **Alternate key** | A candidate key that was not chosen as primary. | cnic, email |
| **Foreign key** | Attribute(s) in one table that reference the primary key of another. | section referencing a SECTION table |
| **Composite key** | A key made of two or more attributes. | ISBN and copy number for BOOKCOPY |

<DbKeyHierarchyFigure />

The lab also showed the trap that costs the most marks: **name and section together are unique in the six rows shown, and they are still not a key.** Uniqueness in the current data is evidence; a key is a promise the business rules make about every state the table can ever be in. Rule 4 breaks that promise.

### Foreign keys, and how they meet participation

Slide 63's foreign key rules connect back to section 3. A foreign key value must match an existing primary key value, or be NULL (**referential integrity**). A foreign key may be NULL **if the relationship is optional**, which is partial participation. If every employee must work for a department (total participation), the dept_no foreign key in EMPLOYEE is declared NOT NULL. And if the referenced primary key is composite, the foreign key must include **all** of its attributes.

<Callout type="exam" title="The deck uses “secondary key” in two different senses">
Slide 62 says: "**An Alternative Key (also called a Secondary Key)** is a candidate key that is not chosen as the primary key." The same deck's summary slide says: "**Secondary Keys: We access on something not necessarily unique.**"

Those contradict each other, and the summary slide is Elmasri's meaning: a **secondary key** is an attribute you search or index on that need not be unique (looking students up by name). An **alternate key** is always unique. If a question says "according to the lecture", write "alternate (secondary) key: a candidate key not chosen as primary". Otherwise say **alternate key** and never call it secondary.
</Callout>

<Callout type="exam" title="“A Composite Key is a Primary Key of two or more columns”">
Slide 64 defines a composite key as a primary key with two or more columns. More precisely, **any** key (super, candidate, primary or foreign) with more than one attribute is composite; the slide's own next line admits "a composite key can also act as a foreign key". Reproduce the slide's sentence if asked for the lecture's definition, and add "any key made of two or more attributes" for the general case.

Do not confuse it with a **composite attribute** (Name made of First and Last, drawn as an oval with child ovals). A composite key is several attributes used together to identify; a composite attribute is one attribute made of parts.
</Callout>

<Deeper kind="exam" title="Model answer: define the types of keys with an example (6 marks)">

Take STUDENT(reg_no, cnic, email, name, section), where reg_no, cnic and email are each unique.

1. **Super key:** any set of attributes that uniquely identifies each student, possibly with extra attributes. Example: reg_no and name.
2. **Candidate key:** a minimal super key; removing any attribute destroys uniqueness. Here reg_no, cnic and email are each candidate keys.
3. **Primary key:** the candidate key chosen by the designer to identify entities; it cannot be NULL. Here reg_no.
4. **Alternate key:** a candidate key not chosen as primary. Here cnic and email.
5. **Foreign key:** an attribute in one table referencing another table's primary key, enforcing referential integrity. Example: ENROLLMENT.reg_no references STUDENT.reg_no.
6. **Composite key:** a key of two or more attributes. Example: reg_no and course_code in ENROLLMENT.

Close with the hierarchy in one line: primary ⊂ candidate ⊂ super.

</Deeper>

<Deeper kind="exam" title="Model answer: explain cardinality ratio and participation with examples (5 marks)">

The **cardinality ratio** of a binary relationship gives the maximum number of relationship instances an entity can participate in: **1:1** (PERSON holds PASSPORT), **1:N** (DEPARTMENT has many EMPLOYEEs, each employee in one department), **M:N** (STUDENT enrolls in many COURSEs, each course has many students). In Chen notation the numbers are written on the lines, each beside the entity it counts.

**Participation** gives the minimum. **Total** participation (existence dependency) means every entity must take part, drawn as a double line: every SECTION must have a TEACHER. **Partial** means some may not, drawn as a single line: a TEACHER may teach no section this term.

Together they are the **structural constraints** of the relationship. Draw one small diagram showing both, and label the double line.

</Deeper>
