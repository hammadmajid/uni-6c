## Where Wireshark sits

<SnifferPlacement />

Two consequences of that picture, both examinable. Wireshark is **passive**: it receives copies, so nothing is slowed, altered or blocked, and neither the browser nor the server can tell it is running. And it captures **frames**, not messages: every row in the list is an Ethernet frame, and the HTTP you care about is buried three headers deep inside a few of them.

<Callout type="exam" title="Say it the way the manual does">
A packet sniffer has two parts: the **packet capture library**, which receives a copy of every link-layer frame sent or received by the computer, and the **packet analyzer**, which displays the contents of every field in every protocol message. Wireshark's window has **five major components**: command menus, display filter, packet listing, packet header details, packet contents in hexadecimal and ASCII. Lab vivas ask for both lists.
</Callout>

<Deeper kind="beyond" title="What you can and cannot see from one laptop">

On a modern switched LAN you see only frames to or from your own interface, plus broadcast and multicast (that is why ARP and mDNS from other devices show up but the TV's Netflix stream does not). Old hubs and the manual's "promiscuous mode" advice come from a time when every frame reached every port.

Wi-Fi is stranger: the driver hands Wireshark Ethernet-shaped frames, so wlp0s20f3 looks like a wired capture. To see real 802.11 headers and other stations' traffic you need monitor mode, which most laptop chipsets support badly.

On a server you rarely run the GUI. Capture with `tcpdump -i eth0 -w cap.pcap port 80`, copy the file down, open it in Wireshark. In Docker, `nicolaka/netshoot` with `--net container:NAME` gives you tcpdump inside another container's network namespace. Same frames, same five panes.

</Deeper>
