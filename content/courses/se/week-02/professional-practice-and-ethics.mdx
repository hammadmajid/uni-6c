Software engineering is not just a technical activity; it carries responsibilities the way medicine or civil engineering does, because the software you write can lose people money, leak their data, or in some domains hurt them. This lesson is the "professional" half of the lecture title: what it means to be a professional, and where the ethical lines sit.

## What makes it a profession

A profession is more than a paid skill. It implies **specialised knowledge**, a duty of **competence**, and accountability to people who **cannot check your work themselves**. A patient cannot audit a surgeon; a bank customer cannot audit the transaction code. Because others rely on your judgement, the judgement itself becomes a responsibility, not just the output.

Sommerville frames this as: software engineers must behave in an **honest and ethically responsible way** to be respected as professionals, going **beyond simply applying the law** because the law lags technology.

## The four issues of professional responsibility

Sommerville names four areas of professional responsibility. Read them as duties that survive whatever your employer asks:

- **Confidentiality**: respect the confidentiality of employers and clients whether or not a formal agreement was signed.
- **Competence**: do not accept work that is beyond your competence, and do not misrepresent your level of skill.
- **Intellectual property rights**: understand and respect patents, copyright and licensing; do not violate them for an employer's convenience.
- **Computer misuse**: do not use your skills to misuse other people's computers, from the trivial (running games on an employer's machine) to the serious (deploying malware or unauthorised access).

<Callout type="key-idea">

The theme across all four: your responsibility is **personal and does not transfer**. "My manager told me to" is not a defence for shipping known-unsafe code, pirating a library, or accessing data you should not. Being an employee constrains how you act on a concern; it does not remove the concern.

</Callout>

## The ACM/IEEE code of ethics

The professional bodies (ACM and IEEE Computer Society) publish a shared **Software Engineering Code of Ethics**. You do not need it verbatim, but you should know its spine: **eight principles**, in a deliberate priority order.

| # | Principle | One line |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Public** | Act consistently with the public interest, above all else |
| 2 | **Client and employer** | Act in their best interests, consistent with the public interest |
| 3 | **Product** | Ensure products meet the highest professional standards |
| 4 | **Judgment** | Maintain integrity and independence in professional judgement |
| 5 | **Management** | Managers promote an ethical approach to the work |
| 6 | **Profession** | Advance the integrity and reputation of the profession |
| 7 | **Colleagues** | Be fair to and supportive of colleagues |
| 8 | **Self** | Keep learning; practise ethical self-improvement |

The order is the message: **the public interest sits above the client and employer**. That is precisely what resolves the opening dilemma.

## Reasoning about a dilemma

The codes are not a lookup table; they are a way to argue. A workable method for exam scenarios:

1. Name the **duties in tension** (e.g. employer's schedule vs public's safety).
2. Apply the **priority order** (public first, then client/employer, then product quality).
3. Choose the action that honours the higher duty **while doing the least harm to the lower** (escalate in writing, propose a safe fast path, refuse only the unsafe part).

<Deeper kind="exam" title="Model answer: is whistle-blowing ever justified? (5 marks)">

Structure it: (1) **State the tension**: an engineer's duty of confidentiality and loyalty to the employer against the duty to the public. (2) **Apply the code**: principle 1 (public) outranks principle 2 (employer), so where the public faces real, serious harm the duty to the public can override confidentiality. (3) **Qualify it**: whistle-blowing is a last resort, justified only after internal escalation has failed, when the harm is serious and the evidence is sound; done recklessly it breaches the duty to colleagues and the profession (principles 6 and 7). (4) **Conclude**: it is justified when serious public harm is otherwise unavoidable and internal channels are exhausted, not as a first move. Cite a real case (e.g. a safety-critical defect knowingly shipped) for the extra mark.

</Deeper>
