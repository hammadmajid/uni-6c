## What a process model adds

The four generic activities tell you *what* has to happen. A **process model** decides the **order, overlap and repetition**: run the activities once in a line, or interleave and repeat them, or assemble from existing parts. Same ingredients, different recipe. The slide lists the common models as waterfall, iterative, incremental, V, spiral and agile; this course focuses on Sommerville's three fundamentals, starting here.

The **SDLC** (software development life cycle) is the umbrella term for this: a process used by industry to design, develop and test high-quality software that meets customer expectations within time and cost. Every model below is one shape of the SDLC.

## The waterfall model

The waterfall model is a **sequential** approach: each fundamental activity is a **separate phase**, arranged in **linear order**. A phase must finish and sign off its work product before the next begins, so progress flows one way, like water down a cascade.

<SeWaterfallModel />

The dashed arrows are the only feedback the pure model allows: in practice you may step **back one phase** to fix something, but the model is built on the assumption that you rarely need to. Going from a testing failure all the way back to a requirements mistake is exactly the expensive path it handles badly.

## When to use it, and when not to

The slide is explicit about the fit: waterfall should **only be applied when requirements are well understood and unlikely to change radically** during development, because its rigid structure makes change hard once the process is underway.

**Advantages (from the slide):**

- Simple and easy to understand and use.
- Easy to manage: each phase has specific deliverables and a review process.
- Phases are processed and completed one at a time.
- Works well for smaller projects where requirements are very well understood.

**Disadvantages (from the slide):**

- Once you are in testing, it is very hard to go back and change something poorly understood at the concept stage.
- No working software is produced until **late** in the life cycle.
- High amounts of risk and uncertainty.
- Poor for complex, object-oriented, long or ongoing projects.
- Not suitable where requirements are at moderate-to-high risk of changing.

<Callout type="key-idea">

The two disadvantages that matter most, and that the exam probes: **no working software until late** (so problems surface when they are expensive) and **change is costly** (so it assumes stable requirements). Every later model is an attempt to fix one of these two.

</Callout>
