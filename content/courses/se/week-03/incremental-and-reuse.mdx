Waterfall's two sins were late feedback and costly change. The other two fundamental models each attack one of them: **incremental development** delivers early and often, and **integration and configuration** avoids writing the risky code at all. This lesson does both, then places the slide's other models (iterative, V, spiral, prototype) around them.

## Incremental development

Sommerville's definition: incremental development is based on developing an **initial implementation**, exposing it to **user feedback**, and **evolving it through several versions** until an acceptable system is built. The three activities (specification, development, validation) do not run once in a line; they **interleave** and repeat.

<SeIncrementalModel />

Each increment ships a **real, running version**. Early increments carry the most important or most urgently needed functionality, so the customer has something usable quickly and every later increment folds in what they learned from the last. This is the opposite of waterfall's "nothing runs until the end".

**Advantages:** early delivery of working software; cheaper to accommodate changing requirements (you only rework the current increment); user feedback continuously steers the product; risk is spread across increments instead of concentrated at the end.

**Disadvantages:** the process is less visible (fewer formal documents per increment, so managers see less), and the system structure can **degrade** as increment after increment is bolted on without periodic refactoring, hurting maintainability.

## Integration and configuration (reuse-oriented)

The third model attacks cost from a different angle: **do not write most of the code**. In the **reuse-oriented** model, the system is **assembled** from existing software: off-the-shelf components, configurable application systems (think a CMS, an ERP, cloud services), and libraries.

<SeReuseModel />

The stage that makes this model distinctive is **requirements refinement**: because you are constrained by what existing components can already do, you **bend the requirements to fit the components**, rather than building components to fit the requirements. You have done this every time you accepted a framework's conventions instead of fighting them.

**Advantages:** less software to write, so faster delivery and lower cost and risk. **Disadvantages:** you give up control, because the requirements compromises may not give users exactly what they wanted, and you are exposed to the evolution of components you do not own.

## Which model, and when

Put the three on two axes: how stable the requirements are, and how soon you need to deliver.

<SeModelFit />

- **Waterfall**: stable, well-understood requirements; a single, late delivery is acceptable.
- **Incremental**: volatile requirements; you need working software early and often to steer the product.
- **Integration and configuration**: suitable off-the-shelf parts exist, and you can bend requirements to use them.

The slide's other models slot in as combinations of these instincts:

| Model (on the slide) | One-line placement |
|---|---|
| **Iterative** | Build all features thinly, then deepen them pass after pass; the sibling of incremental |
| **V model** | Waterfall bent into a V, pairing each phase with the test level that verifies it |
| **Prototype** | Build a throwaway or evolving mock-up to pin down unclear requirements, then build for real |
| **Spiral** | Repeated loops of plan, risk analysis, engineering and customer evaluation, with risk analysis front and centre |
